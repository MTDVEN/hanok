STEP 00

The reference images

no style step

Custom styles sit behind a higher Recraft tier than this account has, so there is no style to build. Attach the matching art/village/ref/matted/ref-*.png as an image reference on every building generation instead.

That is a smaller loss than it sounds. The per-generation reference was always the thing that actually held the viewing angle — wording never did it alone. What a style bought on top was agreement across the set, and that now gets checked by eye instead: accept one building, then hold every later one against it. If they stop agreeing after three or four rolls each, take the one-generation fallback in step 06 — it buys the agreement back in a single image.

Use the matted/ versions, not the raw crops

node tools/refmat.js floats each building on flat mid-grey, feathers away the rectangular crop edge so there is no box for the model to copy, and scales it to exactly the frame fill its own prompt asks for. The raw crops sit the building at ~60% of frame against prompts asking for 25–46% — and a reference wins that argument every time.

Never reference hand drawn reference.jpg or the four journey paintings

The old instruction was to build the style from them, and it is what broke the last set. Those are close-up, near-eye-level architectural studies, and the rejected b-*.png files are faithful reproductions of them — the model did exactly what it was told. The matted crops make its target “a small pale hanok seen from above” instead.

STEP 01

The background plate

image-to-image

Run this image-to-image from valley0.png at a strength clearly lower than feels right. The whole job is to touch the valley floor and nothing else.

This one is opaque on purpose — it is the ground everything else sits on. Transparency toggle off, and do not run it through prep.html.

What v1 got wrong, and the tell to watch

The first roll asked for ~20 clearings and 15+ spurs and delivered exactly that: two near-mirrored halves of path, clearings scattered as even pale discs with no spur attached — a circuit board. Worse, the strength was high enough to regenerate the whole frame. The mist between the ridges went, the mountains flattened into a back wall, the bowl became a plain and the celadon went bright green. Held against valley0.png, the original won.

The tell is never the paths — it is the mountains. If the ridges, the mist or the green have changed at all, the strength is too high, whatever the floor looks like.

Judging the plate — in this order

  • The mountains, the mist and the green. Hold it against valley0.png. Changed? Strength too high, re-roll lower. Nothing about the floor is worth losing the atmosphere for.
  • Symmetry. Do the two halves mirror each other? Re-roll — worn paths never do.
  • The clearings. Do they sit on the paths, or float in the grass? Floating ones are polka dots and read as a diagram.
  • The trunk enters at the bottom centre — the journey's road arrives there, and the gate stands on it.
  • Clearings sit below the middle. Anything higher gets cropped, then feathered away.
  • Don't count them. A network that reads as village lanes beats an accurate one. Spare clearings are the feature: an empty pad beside a full one is what makes the field read as room to grow.

If two lower-strength rolls still drift

Stop re-rolling and switch to the area-edit brush on valley0.png: mask just the meadow floor and paint the lanes in, one at a time. A brush cannot drift by construction — that is the same reason patch.js exists. Slower, and it keeps the painting you already like.

STEP 03

The six buildings

generate in this order

The first and last paragraphs are near-identical across all six on purpose — that repetition is what makes them agree with each other and with the plate. Don't trim or reword it per building. Every one carries the transparency clause, highlighted below.

The frame-fill percentage at the end of each is the building's real size, not a composition preference. All six are painted at the same apparent distance, so the compound doesn't get more detail per metre than the hut. Don't equalise them, and don't rescale afterwards in an editor — that reintroduces exactly the density mismatch that killed the last set.

STEP 04

Transparency — all three, every time

  • Turn Recraft's transparent-background toggle ON. It controls the alpha channel.
  • Keep the background clause in the prompt anyway. The toggle controls alpha; the prompt controls what gets painted. A model that paints a patch of grass hands you grass at full opacity.
  • Run every export through tools/prep.html regardless. That is what trims each building so it stands on the bottom edge of its own canvas — the contract the layout engine depends on.

If the toggle fails, the fallback background is flat MID GREY — never cream or parchment. prep.html keys by flooding inward from the border, and cream sits so close to the hanji walls that the tolerance has to be tight, leaving a rim. Grey is a colour no part of the building uses, so it keys clean.

If Recraft's own Remove Background is available, prefer it over prep.html's keyer — it's segmentation, so it can also clear enclosed holes, which a flood fill structurally cannot. prep.html detects existing alpha and skips keying automatically, so matted and un-matted exports can be dropped in together.

The shadow goes in the PNG, not in code. A CSS shadow follows the alpha silhouette, so it would trace the flared eave tips as a dark fringe in mid-air. That is why every prompt asks for it soft, tight to the base and nearly symmetrical: prep.html trims at the lowest visible pixel, so the shadow's bottom edge becomes the ground line — and roughly 45% of buildings get mirrored, which mirrors the shadow with them.

STEP 08

Village life

two extra generations

Everything above comes first. This is only worth doing while the custom style is already built and loaded — walking villagers and birds over the finished valley. Full build plan in PLAN.html.

The birds need no art at all

At the size a bird reads on this page it is two ink strokes — a flick, not a picture — and a painted PNG cannot flap its wings. They get drawn in code, in the same ink, over the plate. If they come back looking wrong the fallback is a three-pose bird sheet in this style, and the swap costs nothing.

Eight figures in two poses, not sixteen generations

Eight separate rolls would never agree with each other. One sheet is the same trick the “if nothing agrees” fallback recommends for the six buildings — except here it is the first choice, because these figures are far too small for per-image tuning to buy anything. Sheet A is the left leg forward, sheet B the right; alternating them is the walk cycle. The code adds the bob and the sway on top.

At the shipped plate width a hanok is 130–190px across, so a villager lands at roughly 10 × 25px — about 4 × 9px on a phone. Silhouette and tone are the whole game; detail is a liability. That also means the “never rescale afterwards” rule from step 03 does not apply here: these are generated large and shrunk hard on purpose, because nothing at 25px survives being painted at 25px.
